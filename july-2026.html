Ten developments in AI policy this month

The AI policy landscape shifts constantly, and keeping pace with it is part of the job for anyone working in this field. It matters just as much to be able to explain what's happening in plain language that a client or colleague without a legal or technical background can actually follow. That's what this digest is for. It's a single monthly round-up of the developments worth knowing, drawn only from reputable sources. Every item carries a Harvard-style reference so you can verify it yourself, keep a human in the loop, and dig deeper wherever you want.

European Union

Regulation & Law
01

EU AI Act transparency rules take effect

The European Union's AI Act has reached a significant milestone, with rules requiring companies to clearly label AI-generated content such as chatbots and deepfakes now officially in force. Businesses operating in the EU must be transparent when users are interacting with AI rather than humans, and must mark AI-generated images or videos accordingly. These transparency rules are designed to protect everyday people from being misled online, and represent a major step in the EU's broader effort to regulate AI across the region.

(The Verge, 2026a)
02

AI Act reaches social media creators

The EU AI Act is now affecting social media creators and influencers who use AI tools in their content, with transparency requirements meaning they may need to disclose when their content is AI-generated. This shows how broad AI regulations can reach into everyday digital life, not just the operations of large technology companies. The development illustrates the real-world impact of sweeping legislation as it moves from policy text into practice.

(Wired, 2026a)

United States

National Strategy
03

OpenAI staff fund rival political committee

OpenAI employees are funding a political action committee that opposes one backed by their own company's president, Greg Brockman, revealing a significant political divide within one of the world's most powerful AI companies. PACs are organisations that raise money to influence elections and policy, and this internal split concerns how AI should be governed and who should shape related policies. The growing involvement of major AI company figures in political funding is becoming an important channel through which AI policy gets shaped in the United States.

(Wired, 2026b)
04

Anthropic urges states to regulate AI faster

Anthropic, one of the leading AI companies, is actively encouraging US states to pass laws regulating AI more quickly, having supported major AI transparency laws in California and New York. Its policy chief is now warning that even those recent laws may already be out of date given how fast AI is advancing. This is unusual because it shows a major AI company pushing for government oversight rather than resisting it, signalling growing concern about the pace of AI development.

(Wired, 2026c)
05

Inside Trump's AI policy decision-makers

An investigation into AI policy decisions within the Trump administration reveals the people and competing viewpoints shaping the United States' approach to artificial intelligence. The many competing interests involved help explain why US AI policy can be complex and at times inconsistent, as different factions within the administration push in different directions. Understanding who influences government AI policy matters because these decisions affect how AI is regulated, developed, and used across the country.

(Wired, 2026d)
06

xAI sues Minnesota over deepfake content ban

Elon Musk's AI company xAI is suing the state of Minnesota over a law that bans nudification technology, which refers to tools that create fake explicit images of real people without their consent. The case tests whether states have the legal authority to regulate AI-generated harmful content, and the outcome could shape how far state governments can go in restricting certain AI technologies. It is a significant legal moment in the ongoing debate about who has the power to govern AI-generated content in the United States.

(Associated Press, 2026)
07

Nvidia's open-source alliance excludes rivals

Nvidia has formed an open-source AI alliance that pointedly excludes OpenAI and Anthropic, bringing the open versus closed source debate in AI into sharp focus alongside discussions about who shapes White House AI policy. The tension between open and closed AI systems carries real regulatory implications, as governments must decide whether to encourage or restrict open-source AI development. The story reflects how corporate alliances are becoming a new front in the broader contest over AI governance and policy direction in Washington.

(Wired, 2026e)

Global

Safety & Standards
08

DeepMind CEO proposes independent AI regulator

The CEO of Google DeepMind is calling for a new independent organisation, similar to the watchdog bodies that oversee financial industries, to test the most powerful AI systems and set rules for how they should be released to the public. This is a significant policy proposal because it comes from a leading AI company executive who is suggesting the industry needs outside oversight rather than self-regulation alone. If such a body were created, it could shape global standards for how the most advanced AI is developed and deployed.

(TechCrunch, 2026a)
09

AI safety warnings grow harder to dismiss

An OpenAI test in which AI models were evaluated for cybersecurity capabilities produced notable and concerning behaviours, adding to a growing body of evidence that AI safety is a genuine governance challenge. AI safety is a core part of technology governance debates because it directly informs how governments and companies should regulate and oversee AI systems. Stories like this contribute to the public and policy conversation about whether AI development is outpacing the safety measures designed to contain it.

(The Verge, 2026b)
10

Open-weight AI closing gap with safety risks

A new report from SaferAI found that powerful open-weight AI models, whose underlying code is publicly shared, are getting close to matching the most advanced AI systems in capability but without the same safety protections in place. This raises an important policy concern: if open models become very powerful but lack safety guardrails, existing governance frameworks may not be equipped to oversee them. The finding is central to one of the key debates in AI regulation, namely how to handle AI tools that anyone can freely download and modify.

(TechCrunch, 2026b)
