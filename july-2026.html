AI Policy, Explained

Issue 01 ● July 2026 ● By Mariyan Silva
The AI policy landscape shifts constantly, and keeping pace with it is part of the job for anyone working in this field. It matters just as much to be able to explain what's happening in plain language that a client or colleague without a legal or technical background can actually follow. That's what this digest is for. It's a single monthly round-up of the developments worth knowing, drawn only from reputable sources. Every item carries a Harvard-style reference so you can verify it yourself, keep a human in the loop, and dig deeper wherever you want.

EU

Regulation & Law

The European Union's AI Act has reached a meaningful new milestone: transparency rules requiring companies to clearly label AI-generated content, including chatbots and deepfakes, are now officially in force. Businesses operating in the EU must now be upfront when users are interacting with AI rather than a human, and must mark AI-generated images or videos as such. These measures are designed to protect ordinary people from being misled online, and they represent a significant step forward in the EU's broader ambition to regulate artificial intelligence across the region. (The Verge, 2026a)

Beyond the headline labelling obligations, the EU AI Act's reach is proving broader than many anticipated, extending into the world of social media creators and influencers who use AI tools in their content. The Act's transparency requirements mean that creators may need to disclose when their output is AI-generated, a development that moves regulation firmly into everyday digital life rather than confining it to large technology companies. For anyone advising clients who produce online content in Europe, this is an area worth watching closely. (Wired, 2026a)

US

National Strategy

A detailed look inside the Trump administration reveals the individuals and competing viewpoints currently shaping the United States' approach to artificial intelligence. With what one observer described as "10 sides" to every AI policy question, it becomes easier to understand why US AI policy can appear complex and sometimes contradictory. Knowing who holds influence over these decisions matters, because those decisions affect how AI is regulated, developed, and used across the country. (Wired, 2026b)

Nvidia has launched an open-source alliance that notably excludes both OpenAI and Anthropic, reflecting a deepening divide in the industry over whether AI development should be open or closed. The open versus closed source debate carries genuine regulatory implications, as governments must decide whether to encourage or restrict the public availability of powerful AI systems. White House deliberations over this question are ongoing, making it one of the more consequential threads in current US AI policy. (Wired, 2026e)

Industry Response

A political divide within OpenAI has surfaced in an unusual way: employees are funding a political action committee that directly opposes one backed by their own company's president, Greg Brockman. PACs are organisations that raise money to influence elections and policy, and their increasing use by figures connected to major AI companies is becoming a notable feature of how AI governance is contested in the United States. The episode points to genuine disagreement, even within a single organisation, about who should shape AI policy and in which direction. (Wired, 2026c)

Anthropic is taking an unusual stance for a leading AI company: it is actively encouraging US states to pass AI regulation more quickly, having supported major transparency laws in both California and New York. Its policy chief is now warning that even those recently enacted laws may already be out of date, given the pace at which AI capabilities are advancing. The willingness of a frontier AI company to push for tighter government oversight, rather than resist it, signals a growing internal concern about whether the industry is moving faster than the rules designed to govern it. (Wired, 2026d)

Regulation & Law

Elon Musk's AI company xAI has filed a lawsuit against the state of Minnesota over a law banning so-called "nudification" technology, which refers to tools that generate fake explicit images of real people without their consent. The case is significant because it tests the extent to which state governments have the legal authority to restrict specific categories of AI-generated harmful content. The outcome could set an important precedent for how far states can go in regulating AI tools, at a time when federal legislation on the subject remains limited. (Associated Press, 2026a)

Global

Industry Response

Google DeepMind's chief executive is calling for the creation of a new independent organisation to test the most powerful AI systems and set standards for how they are released to the public, drawing an analogy to the watchdog bodies that oversee financial markets. The proposal is notable precisely because it comes from inside one of the world's leading AI companies, with its own executive suggesting the sector needs external oversight rather than relying on self-regulation alone. Were such a body to be established, it could have far-reaching consequences for how frontier AI is developed and governed at a global level. (TechCrunch, 2026a)

Safety & Standards

An OpenAI evaluation involving AI models tested for cybersecurity capabilities has drawn fresh attention to whether AI safety measures are keeping pace with the capabilities being built into these systems. AI safety sits at the heart of technology governance debates, directly informing how governments and companies decide to oversee AI development. Episodes like this one feed into the broader policy conversation about whether the speed of AI development is outrunning the frameworks meant to make it safe. (The Verge, 2026b)

A report from SaferAI has found that open-weight AI models, whose underlying code is shared publicly for anyone to download and modify, are now approaching the capability levels of the most advanced closed systems, yet without the same safety protections in place. This raises a pointed regulatory question: if open models become highly capable but lack guardrails, existing governance frameworks may struggle to keep up. How to handle freely available AI tools is one of the central dilemmas in current AI policy, and this report gives that debate fresh urgency. (TechCrunch, 2026b)
