AI Policy, Explained

Issue 01·July 2026·By Mariyan Silva

The AI policy landscape shifts constantly, and keeping pace with it is part of the job for anyone working in this field. It matters just as much to be able to explain what's happening in plain language that a client or colleague without a legal or technical background can actually follow. That's what this digest is for. It's a single monthly round-up of the developments worth knowing, drawn only from reputable sources. Every item carries a Harvard-style reference so you can verify it yourself, keep a human in the loop, and dig deeper wherever you want.

EU

Regulation & Law

A significant milestone arrived this month for the EU's AI Act, with transparency rules now officially in force across the bloc. Businesses must clearly indicate when users are interacting with an AI rather than a human, and AI-generated images or videos must be marked as such, making it harder for people to be misled online. This represents a concrete step in the EU's broader ambition to bring robust, enforceable standards to AI across the region (The Verge, 2026a).

Beyond large technology companies, the reach of the EU AI Act is also being felt by social media creators and influencers who use AI tools in their content. The law's transparency requirements mean that creators may need to disclose when their posts, videos, or images are AI-generated, extending regulatory expectations into everyday digital life. This development illustrates how broad the Act's practical scope is, touching not just corporate actors but individual content producers too (Wired, 2026a).

US

National Strategy

Inside the Trump administration, AI policy is being shaped by a wide cast of advisers with competing views, making the United States' overall approach to AI governance genuinely difficult to read. Understanding who holds influence matters because these decisions determine how AI is regulated, developed, and deployed across the country. The many competing interests involved help explain why US AI policy can appear fragmented or inconsistent (Wired, 2026b).

Adding another layer to the strategic picture, Nvidia has launched an open-source AI alliance that pointedly excludes OpenAI and Anthropic, drawing attention to the deepening tensions between open and closed approaches to AI development. Whether governments encourage or restrict open-source AI has genuine regulatory implications, since freely available models can be downloaded and modified by anyone, complicating oversight efforts. White House discussions about AI policy are directly shaped by where key players stand in this debate (Wired, 2026e).

Industry Response

A notable internal fault line has opened at OpenAI, with some employees funding a political action committee that directly opposes one backed by the company's own president, Greg Brockman. PACs are organisations that raise money to influence elections and public policy, and their use by AI company figures is a growing feature of how AI governance gets shaped in the US. The split signals a genuine political divide within one of the most powerful AI companies over the direction of AI governance (Wired, 2026c).

Anthropic is taking an unusual stance for a leading AI company: actively encouraging US states to move faster on AI regulation rather than lobbying to slow it down. The company's policy team supported major transparency laws in California and New York, but is now warning that even those recently passed measures may already be outdated given how quickly AI capabilities are advancing. This posture, pushing for more oversight rather than less, reflects a growing concern within parts of the industry about whether governance is keeping pace with the technology (Wired, 2026d).

Regulation & Law

Elon Musk's AI company xAI has filed a lawsuit against the state of Minnesota over a law banning so-called "nudification" technology, which creates fake explicit images of real people without their consent. The case is significant because it tests the legal authority of state governments to restrict particular AI-generated content, and the outcome could set a precedent for how far states can go in regulating harmful AI applications. Whichever way it is decided, the case is likely to inform future legislative efforts at both state and federal level (Associated Press, 2026).

Global

Industry Response

Google DeepMind's chief executive is calling for a new independent body, modelled on the kind of watchdog organisations that oversee financial industries, to test the most powerful AI systems and establish rules governing their public release. The proposal is notable precisely because it comes from a senior executive at a leading AI company, suggesting that the industry itself is beginning to recognise the limits of self-regulation. If such a body were established, it could play a defining role in setting global standards for how frontier AI is developed and deployed (TechCrunch, 2026a).

Safety & Standards

A report published by SaferAI this month found that open-weight AI models, those whose underlying code is publicly shared and freely downloadable, are rapidly closing the capability gap with the most advanced proprietary systems, while the safety gap between them remains wide. This creates a pressing policy concern: governance frameworks designed with commercial, closed models in mind may simply not be equipped to oversee powerful AI tools that anyone can modify and redistribute. The findings are likely to intensify the regulatory debate about how to handle open-source AI development (TechCrunch, 2026b).

Concerns about AI safety received fresh attention following an OpenAI evaluation in which AI models were tested for cybersecurity capabilities and displayed behaviours that prompted wider commentary. Safety evaluation is a core element of technology governance, directly informing how policymakers and companies decide to regulate and oversee AI systems. Episodes of this kind contribute to the growing public and policy debate about whether AI development is moving faster than the safety measures designed to contain it (The Verge, 2026b).
